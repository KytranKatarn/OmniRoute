Autumn 2026 municipal cycle

Four provinces vote for councils and mayors within three weeks: British Columbia on 17 October, Ontario on 26 October, Manitoba on 28 October, Québec on 1 November. Municipal government spends the money closest to people and is covered by the fewest reporters, which makes this the densest and thinnest-covered stretch of the whole cycle.

Who is running

Mayors and ward councillors both. A ward decides the zoning on one street; a mayor does not.

A by-election is called, not scheduled

Every other date in this section is fixed by statute. A by-election is not: when a seat falls vacant the Speaker reports it to the Chief Electoral Officer, and the writ must then issue inside a legal window — federally, between 11 and 180 days. Where in that window the vote lands is the government’s choice, which is why a vacancy can sit for five months and still be entirely lawful.

The dates that actually matter

Election day is the last deadline, not the first. Registration closes before it, advance voting opens before it, and nomination close is the moment a slate becomes final — which is when a race becomes fully reportable and when a district with no candidate becomes a story rather than a wait.

Registered electors · the denominator

A turnout percentage is a fraction, and the register underneath it grows every year. More people voting in raw numbers can still mean a lower rate — which is exactly what happened in 2021.

Did coverage follow the campaign, or the calendar?

For each city that has already voted: articles per week in the four weeks before, the week of the vote, and the four weeks after. A tall middle bar with almost nothing either side means the press arrived for election day and left — which is coverage of an event, not of a government.

Why there are no polls here

Aggregating horse-race numbers would put this newsroom in the prediction business, and a site whose premise is that framing shapes perception should not spend the campaign telling people who is winning. What is published instead is retrospective and checkable: how much each race was covered, what turnout followed, and who actually won. Coverage against outcome is a comparison anyone can verify after the fact. A projection is not.

Graded on municipal stories actually filed, in absolute counts. A city with a live feed and no council coverage is not covered — but a small town with one story is not better covered than Toronto with twelve, which is what a per-resident measure would claim.

How this grade is built, and two versions we threw away

Cities are graded on absolute municipal story counts in the window. None is no story. Minimal is one or two — enough to know a council exists, not enough to follow it. Some is three to nine. Sustained is ten or more, which is roughly one a week. Stories per 100,000 residents is shown beside each city as context, and is deliberately not the threshold.

Version one marked a city ready if any source filed anything inside a freshness window. That returned 30 of 31 and read as reassurance — it measured whether we had a feed, not whether anyone was watching a council.

Version two graded on stories per 100,000 residents. That inverted the thing it was measuring: Churchill, population 870, would clear the threshold on a single story, while Toronto would need twenty-eight to reach the same grade. News coverage does not scale linearly with population — one competent reporter can cover one council, whether the city has 900 residents or 900,000. Every province came out at 0%, and a measure that cannot distinguish between them is not a measure.

Three things this version still cannot see, all of which mean the real picture is worse rather than better. It cannot tell a campaign story from any other council story, so a rezoning report counts as election coverage. It cannot tell whether a reporter attended anything or rewrote a release. And it counts a story that mentions a city, not one written from it.
