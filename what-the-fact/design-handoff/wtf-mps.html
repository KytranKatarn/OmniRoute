Why the Senate can stop a bill

Senators face no election, so the chamber can take a position the House will not. Most bills pass unchanged; the ones that do not are where the two chambers actually disagree — tracked below.

Where this data comes from

Boundaries, names and the postal-code lookup are read from the Represent API, maintained by OpenNorth from Elections Canada source data. The set loaded here is the representation order Represent currently serves; the House presently seats 343 members, so a redistribution can leave the two briefly out of step.

On the live site this comes from the newsroom database, which also holds the riding-to-member join. That is why no member is named on this page — the mapping is not in this project, and inferring it from geography would put a real person in the wrong seat.

What the coverage counts are not

A high count means a member is written about often. It does not mean they are effective, controversial, or well regarded. Cabinet ministers and party leaders attract coverage by office, so comparing a minister to a backbencher measures the job, not the person.

What is missing here and why. Ridings, provinces, photographs, cabinet roles and real coverage volumes all come from the newsroom database on the live site. This rebuild does not have it, so those fields are left visibly empty instead of filled with something plausible. Article counts shown are sample-corpus figures. On a site that fact-checks named public officials, a guessed biographical detail is the one error that would cost more than an empty field.
