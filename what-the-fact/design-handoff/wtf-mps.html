MPs & ridings

Every member of the House of Commons and the Senate, seated where they actually sit. Coverage counts show how often each is written about — volume, not favour.

House floor plan · 45th Parliament

Seat coordinates and party colours are the live site’s own, scraped by it from ourcommons.ca. Party assignment here is derived from arc position to match published standings, so a single seat’s party may be wrong even where the totals are right. Ridings are shown only for the five members on record; the rest read “not recorded” rather than carrying a guess.

Senate of Canada · the red chamber

Senators are appointed, not elected, and sit until 75. Six caucus groups occupy a horseshoe rather than facing benches, which is why the chamber reads less adversarially than the House.

Source: Senate of Canada, roster as of May 2026. Caucus colours are the live site’s own. 96 seats are mapped and 96 senators are listed — the live page states 96 but renders 93, with three non-affiliated rows missing a region. Regions are not carried here, so every row says so.

What the coverage counts are not

A high count means a member is written about often. It does not mean they are effective, controversial, or well regarded. Cabinet ministers and party leaders attract coverage by office, so comparing a minister to a backbencher measures the job, not the person.

What is missing here and why. Ridings, provinces, photographs, cabinet roles and real coverage volumes all come from the newsroom database on the live site. This rebuild does not have it, so those fields are left visibly empty instead of filled with something plausible. Article counts shown are sample-corpus figures. On a site that fact-checks named public officials, a guessed biographical detail is the one error that would cost more than an empty field.
